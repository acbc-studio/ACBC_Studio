You’ve listened, and it’s sitting in a folder.

When the board asks, “What does the data say?”, your director is in the hot seat, expected to prove the listening was worth it. They turn to you for what matters most, and you’d love to be the one who knows. You just can’t say yet which way it points.

Why it keeps happening

Part of it is how institutions listen. The record gets thinned out into forms, surveys and loose notes that keep the topic but lose what people meant. Even when the person in the room is changed by what they heard, little of that makes it onto the page. Then making sense of it happens afterward, often by one or two people, against a template built before anyone spoke. Stories become themes, themes become averages, and review softens whatever is left.

Listening is half of it. The other half is making sense of what you heard, together.

That’s why the notes point in twenty directions and the logic model stays closed. Without that shared step, there’s no clear sense of what matters most, and no clear path to the goal you already have. Sensemaking builds both with you. A Shared Compass shows where to focus, and a Living Map shows how to get there.

What people told you either shapes the decision, or gets set aside.

The planning template doesn’t decide which. Your team does, in how you listen, how you make sense of what you heard, and how you carry it into the room.

In practice

How one task force stopped funding the wrong fix

I once worked with a food security task force on what’s called the “SNAP gap”: people enrolled in MassHealth who are often eligible for food assistance but weren’t getting it. The assumption was that they didn’t know they qualified, so the original plan was an awareness campaign.

What a Shared Compass does Shows the reasons people gave, which ones matter most, and where to start.

We listened, and the assumption fell apart almost immediately. People knew what SNAP was. For some, the benefit wasn’t worth the paperwork. Others had dropped off because reapplying every year was too much, or because of how they were treated at check-ins. The reasons people gave, not our assumption, became what the task force prioritized its work around.

What a Living Map does Shows where each barrier sits, who controls it, and which parts are yours to change.

Then we mapped the journey to get food assistance and keep it, and marked where each barrier happens and who controls it. “Awareness” wasn’t on it. Yearly reapplication was a state rule, so the task force could help families with the paperwork and push for easier renewals. Check-ins were run by the local office, so it could take what families said straight to that office.

What came of it

So the task force didn’t spend its budget on an awareness campaign people didn’t need. It put that money toward simpler renewals, better check-ins and the local programs neighbors already trust.

How it works

From everything you’ve gathered to a finished figure, in twelve to sixteen weeks.

A Shared Compass takes about twelve weeks. A Living Map can take up to four more, because mapping each step, what’s in the way and who can change it takes longer to build and check. Open any step for the detail.

With your teamWith your communities

  1. Weeks 1–2

    Read and recommend · with your teamA clear read on what you already have.

    You send everything you’ve gathered. I write the readiness memo, covering what it’s saying, what’s missing, and whether a Shared Compass, a Living Map or neither fits yet. If neither does, we stop there, or I may suggest starting with the Un-Learning Series.

  2. Weeks 3–4

    Design together · with your teamA plan for who to hear, and how.

    You tell me who most needs to be heard, how your team can reach them, and what has or hasn’t worked before. I design the listening to fit, whether that’s small group conversations, visits to places people already gather, or a mix, and write a facilitation guide your team can carry into the room.

  3. Weeks 5–7

    Rehearse, then host · with your communitiesYour team in the room, listening.

    Before the first conversation, your team walks through the facilitation guide with me in a rehearsal. I give feedback on the questions, the pacing, and how to handle the moments that get hard. Then your team hosts the conversations, so the trust that gets built stays with the people who’ll keep showing up.

  4. Weeks 8–9

    Make sense and build · with your teamWhat you heard, taking shape.

    We sort through what you heard together in a shared workspace, grouping what people said and naming the patterns in their words, not ours. I turn that into a first sketch. For a Shared Compass, that’s a handful of priorities and why each one matters. A Living Map takes about four more weeks, because we also trace where people get stuck along the way and who can move each barrier.

  5. Weeks 10–12

    Bring back the sketch · with your communitiesThe people you heard from shape it.

    Your team takes the sketch back to the people you heard from and asks whether you heard them right, and what’s missing. Seeing their own words in it, before anything is final, shows people the listening was real. I finish the figure with what they tell you, so the version your organization plans from is one they’ve already recognized as theirs.

    3–4 months later

    Journey Log · with your teamMomentum you can see.

    You tell me what’s moved since the figure was finished, including the early starts that rarely get counted: a new relationship, a little more trust, a clearer sense of what your team can take on. I design a short update, and your team shares it with the people you heard from.

What it won’t fix

A Compass or Map won’t make your work community-led on its own. It won’t change who holds decision-making power, which parts of the work are resourced, or undo a choice made before anyone asked your communities.

But it disrupts what usually happens between listening and the plan.

It makes what community members told you harder to ignore and easier to act on, and shows where money and time should move to follow what they asked for. It puts their words on the table when the decisions get made.

Questions people ask

Before you bring this to your team

How do we know which one we need?

You don’t have to know yet. Everyone starts with the same readiness memo. A Living Map needs more community input than a Compass, because it traces each step of the path, so I’ll tell you what yours can carry. If you have enough for a Map, you can still choose a Compass. If you don’t have enough for either yet, I’ll say so and suggest where to begin.

What if we don’t have a community group?

Many public agencies don’t. My recommended path is to partner with a community organization that already holds the trust of the people the work is for, and pay them as a partner. It adds to your costs, but it builds an ongoing relationship for the initiatives that come next. You can also listen through one-on-ones or at a gathering that already happens, as long as you can come back to the same people.

What if what we hear unsettles our plan?

It probably will, somewhere. That’s usually a sign the listening worked. We won’t bend it to fit, and we won’t pretend you can act on everything at once. We’ll work out together what you can carry into this plan, what you’ll keep pushing for, and how to be honest with your communities about both.

Can this be our strategic plan or community assessment?

No, but it can be where one starts. A strategic plan or community assessment usually covers much more, like goals, strategic actions, budgets, timelines and data across your whole community. A Shared Compass or Living Map is one clear figure of where to go and why, built from the community input you’ve already gathered and are still trying to make sense of. Start with Sensemaking, and the larger plan or assessment can grow from what people told you.

If our team does the listening, what are we paying for?

You’re paying for the parts that are hard to do from the inside, like designing how to listen, making sense of what you heard without flattening it, and building a figure that holds up in the room. I do that work alongside your team. Your team holds the conversations, because those relationships need to outlast the project. You come away knowing how each priority was reached, and with a feel for how to run a lighter version next time.

Will it fit our procurement process?

A Shared Compass is about $9,000, which comes in under the point where many public agencies need formal quotes or bids. A Living Map is about $12,000 to $16,000, depending on scope. Both include the readiness memo and the Journey Log. For a Map, I’m glad to provide a scope of work and a quote for whatever your process needs.

What if we need more than what’s included?

The prices on this page cover what’s listed under What’s included, and nothing more. Anything beyond that list is added scope, and it adds to the cost. That might be hearing from a second group, taking the sketch back more than once, mapping more than one path, coordinating translation, or bringing a facilitator I trust into the room, with their travel covered. We’ll agree on the scope and the cost before anything new begins.

Do you use AI?

Yes, a bit, as a tool. After your team has sorted through what it heard together, AI helps me code and sort it more closely, and helps me illustrate the figure. Nothing I make is ever fully generated by AI, and the quotes you see are always people’s own words. If your team would rather I work without it, please know the process will take longer and cost more. Just ask and I’ll quote it separately.

Let’s turn what you’ve heard into a plan you can stand behind.

You told people their input would matter. Every month it waits, that promise gets harder to keep. The people you heard from come a little less often, the case for your program gets thinner, and the barriers they named stay right where they are.

And there’s usually something coming up, like a new plan, a budget or a grant renewal. What goes into it now is what your organization will be held to for years. Change it afterwards, and you’re undoing work instead of shaping it.

Start now, and the next time someone asks what the data says, you’ll answer in their words, and the people you heard from will see it was worth coming. I’d love to hear about it.
