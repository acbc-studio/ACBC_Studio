Maybe this is where you are right now.

You’ve listened, and it’s sitting in a folder.

The survey responses, the transcripts, the photos of sticky notes from the open house, the comment cards in three languages. Every time someone opens the folder, they close it again.

Your plan template asks for goals and indicators, and a resident told you, “My landlord won’t fix the AC and I’m scared to complain.” Nobody’s sure how to get from one to the other.

And when leadership asks, “What does the data say?”, what your communities told you sounds like anecdote next to the spreadsheet. You know it matters more than that. You just can’t show how much.

What gets in the way

None of this is because you aren’t trying hard enough. Most of it comes from how our institutions were set up to listen.

Engagement gets designed around the grant, not the decision. Surveys average out the people you most need to hear. And plan templates have boxes for goals while people speak in stories, so whatever doesn’t fit gets trimmed, and review softens what’s left.

And you’re the one in the middle. You stood in front of people at the listening session and told them their input would matter, and you’ll see them at the next community meeting when it doesn’t. Your communities notice too. They’ve been surveyed and listening-sessioned for years, and when little comes back, they stop coming, and the institution starts calling them “hard to reach.” They aren’t hard to reach. They’ve been reached plenty, and nothing came back.

You know a plan can carry what people told you into decisions that change their lives, and you know a plan can also be where those things go to wait. That isn’t a sign you’re doing it wrong. It’s what doing this work honestly from inside an institution feels like.

What this looks like in practice

I once worked with a food security task force on what’s called the “SNAP gap”: people enrolled in MassHealth who seemed eligible for SNAP food assistance but weren’t getting it. The assumption was that they didn’t know they qualified, so the plan was outreach.

What a Shared Compass does

Puts the reasons people gave at the center of your plan.

We listened, and the assumption fell apart almost right away. People knew what SNAP was. For some, the benefit wasn’t worth the paperwork. Others had dropped off because reapplying every year was too much, or because of how they were treated at check-ins. The reasons people gave, not our assumption, became what the task force organized its work around.

What a Living Map does

Shows where each barrier sits, who controls it, and which parts are yours to change.

Then we mapped the journey a person takes to get food assistance and keep it, and placed each barrier where it happens. Each one was controlled by someone different, and “awareness” wasn’t on the path at all.

So the task force redrew its role: advocacy on the rules it couldn’t change, a relationship with the Department of Transitional Assistance, training for the people neighbors already trust, and more support for local options like Meals on Wheels and the food bank.

What Sensemaking is

Sensemaking turns what your communities have told you into one clear figure your institution can plan from. Your team listens and builds it with your communities, and I’m alongside you from reading what you’ve already gathered to designing the final figure.

Your communities name the priorities

Each priority keeps a few of their own words beside it, and where they were heard. When leadership asks where it came from, you can show them.

Your communities shape the draft

Your team brings back a rough sketch, not a finished figure, and asks: did we hear this right, and what’s missing? What they say changes the final version.

What it won’t fix

A Compass or Map won’t make your work community-led on its own. It won’t change who holds decision-making power, or undo a choice made before anyone asked your communities.

But it makes what your communities know harder to ignore and easier to act on.

This isn’t for you if

  • You need engagement to justify decisions already made.
  • You want it done entirely for you.
  • You want a logic model only to satisfy a funder.

Questions people ask

Before you bring this to your team

What if we don’t have a community group?

Many public agencies don’t. My recommended path is to partner with a community organization that already holds the trust of the people the work is for, and pay them as a partner. It adds to your costs, but it builds an ongoing relationship for the initiatives that come next. You can also listen through one-on-ones or at a gathering that already happens, as long as you can come back to the same people.

How many community members should we involve?

It depends on how you listen, and the readiness memo will help us decide. For understanding why something isn’t working, a small group often goes further than a broad survey. The people most affected are usually a small share of a whole city, so surveys average their experience out. When you start hearing the same things again and again, you’ve learned what you need, and that often happens sooner than you’d expect.

What if what we hear unsettles our plan?

It probably will, somewhere. That’s usually a sign the listening worked. We won’t bend it to fit, and we won’t pretend you can act on everything at once. We’ll work out together what you can carry into this plan, what you’ll keep pushing for, and how to be honest with your communities about both.

If our team does the listening, what are we paying for?

You’re paying for the parts that are hardest to do well from the inside: designing how to listen so you hear what matters most, making sense of it without flattening it, and building a figure that holds up in the room. I do that work with your team at every step. Your team holds the conversations, because those relationships need to outlast the project. So you come away able to defend every conclusion, and with the know-how to run a lighter version next time.

Will it fit our procurement process?

A Shared Compass is about $9,000, which comes in under the point where many public agencies need formal quotes or bids. A Living Map is about $12,000 to $16,000, depending on scope. Both include the readiness memo and the Journey Log. For a Map, I’m glad to provide a scope of work and a quote for whatever your process needs.
