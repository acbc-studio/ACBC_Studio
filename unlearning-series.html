(Un)Learning Series · Open what’s possible

The part of community engagement your degree didn’t teach.

For people in public health, planning and community development who came to this work to make change, and keep bumping into the limits of how it gets done.

Over twelve weeks, you and the people you work most closely with will examine the top-down habits so many of us picked up along the way, and practice a different relationship with your role, one where you walk alongside communities and answer to them, rather than deciding for them.

The first series opens in fall 2027. Up to three founding teams will help shape it.

Maybe this is where you are right now.

What people told you doesn’t survive the report.

You sat with community members and heard something real. Then it went into a logic model, a template and three rounds of review, and by the end you could feel what got sanded off. When you bring a story to the table, someone asks, “But what does the data say?”

Communities come in after the decision is made.

By the time people are invited in, the plan, the program or the service is already set. You go back to the same neighborhood for another survey and hear, “We already told you.” And just as people start to trust you, the grant ends.

You’re the one carrying it.

Maybe you’re expected to bring “the community voice” because you’re from there, or look like you might be. Maybe you’re the one who keeps asking whose voice is missing. Either way, you’re holding both worlds, and you’re tired of holding them alone.

You’ve done the reading, and Monday looks the same.

You’ve read the books and sat through the trainings, and you agree with all of it. But tomorrow’s meeting runs on the same template, the same workshop and a council or funder who expects it done the usual way.

You came into this work wanting things to be more just, and some days it feels like the job is asking you to manage people instead.

These ways of working were learned, which means they can be unlearned, together. That’s what the (Un) is for.

Where these habits come from

Most of our training taught us to run an assessment, analyze the data and write the plan, then do it all again next cycle. Community engagement, if it came up at all, was a set of tools for gathering input, and rarely a matter of relationships to tend or power to share.

That’s no accident. Many of the institutions that shaped us were built to produce experts who decide on behalf of others, and some of it we absorbed long before any degree, learning whose word counts and who gets to decide. Those lessons land hardest on Black, Indigenous and other communities of color, immigrants, and low-income and queer communities, whose knowledge is so often the first to be dismissed.

Underneath it all are colonial, capitalist and neoliberal ways of seeing the world, so familiar they’re like the water we swim in. Here’s what they look like day to day.

Colonial

Deciding about people, without them

  • Studying the same neighborhoods again and again, while the people who live there rarely decide how their data is used.

Capitalist

Valuing what can be counted, sold or reported

  • Counting units built and people served, while the relationships that made it possible never show up in the report.
  • Watching redlined neighborhoods get “revitalized” once land values rise, along the same lines of race Cedric Robinson called racial capitalism.

Neoliberal

Turning shared conditions into personal problems

  • Asking people to eat better or build an emergency kit when the problem is food access or flood risk.
  • Competing for contracts with the organizations you should be building with, because “that’s just how funding works.”

None of this critique is new. Black feminist, Black radical and Indigenous thinkers and organizers, alongside liberation and decolonial movements, named these patterns long ago, and their ways of knowing, deciding and caring for one another are very much alive today. Our part, from inside our institutions, is to listen closely enough to act on what they teach.

A note from Andréanne

“If what I feel doesn’t count as knowledge, my mind will have to.”

I grew up where science was the closest thing to faith, and went on to work in fields that trusted what could be measured above other ways of knowing. Loosening that hold has taken years, and I’m still practicing. What helped most was learning alongside others, and from Black feminist, Black radical and Indigenous thinkers and communities whose ways of knowing carry on today. So this series is unlearning held together, with every source named.

From expert to steward

At its heart, this series is about a new relationship with our roles. What are we actually responsible for, and who are we accountable to?

What many of us learned to doWhat stewardship asks of us
Target, intervene and manage outcomes→Walk alongside people, and let go of what isn’t ours to control
Gather input, then decide→Share the decisions that matter most
Take stories and data for the report→Make sure knowledge flows back to the people it came from
Count only what fits the frame as evidence→Carry community knowledge whole, with its own rigor
Answer first to funders and leadership→Answer to the people the work is for

What we’ll explore

Each theme runs for three weeks. You start with short video lessons at your own pace, then we meet live to work through them in the context of your own work, and then you take what came up into a practice on your real project. Each live session opens with what you tried last time. Your team brings one real plan, project or decision, so every theme lands on work that’s already on your desk. This is our early outline, and founding teams will help shape it.

Weeks 1–3

Collective care

What would it mean to count care as real work?

We’ll unlearn the belief that output and deadlines come first, which leaves care to after hours and the same few people.

We’ll practice building care into the work, with timelines that follow community rhythms and budgets with room for fair pay and childcare.

You’ll leave with a clear picture of who’s carrying what in your project, and one change to share that load.

Learning from the Combahee River Collective, the Black Panther Party’s survival programs, Sins Invalid and adrienne maree brown.

Weeks 4–6

Living memory

Whose knowledge guides the work, and whose is missing?

We’ll unlearn taking people’s stories and disappearing until the next assessment, and holding all knowledge to one standard of evidence.

We’ll practice staying in relationship between cycles, bringing back what people shared, and honoring lived experience as evidence in its own right.

You’ll leave with the language and sources to stand up for community knowledge in rooms that ask for rigor.

Learning from Linda Tuhiwai Smith, Shawn Wilson, Patricia Hill Collins and Indigenous data sovereignty movements.

Weeks 7–9

Shared power

Who decides now, and who could?

We’ll unlearn the habit of control, from a few people holding the final say to bringing community in once the plan is set.

We’ll practice mapping who decides at each step, and learning from community boards, participatory budgeting and consensus.

You’ll leave with one decision your team is ready to share, and a plan for sharing it.

Learning from Ella Baker, the Zapatistas, Paulo Freire and INCITE!

Weeks 10–12

Radical imagination

What becomes possible when “realistic” isn’t the limit?

We’ll unlearn the single story of progress, and the sense that funders and markets set the limits of what’s possible.

We’ll practice imagining your work differently together, then naming what you can change from inside and how to support community-led work outside your walls.

You’ll leave with your team’s first steps, inside and outside your organization.

Learning from Robin D.G. Kelley, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Mariame Kaba and Faranak Miraftab.

What changes

Twelve weeks from now, here’s what’s different for your team.

  • What people tell you makes it into the work, whole.

    Before Community knowledge trimmed to fit the template, then waved off as anecdote.

    After The language and sources to defend it as evidence, in the rooms that ask for rigor.

  • Communities come in before the decision, not after.

    Before The plan is set, then people are invited to react.

    After At least one real decision in your project, shared with the people it affects.

  • Trust builds across cycles instead of resetting.

    Before Another round of asking, and nothing given back.

    After A habit of reporting back before you ask for more, and relationships that outlast the grant.

  • You’re not carrying it alone anymore.

    Before You’re the bridge, the conscience and the one who always asks.

    After A whole team that asks “whose voice is missing?” and shares the weight of the answer.

  • Care becomes part of the work.

    Before Timelines set by grant cycles, and care squeezed into after hours.

    After Timelines and budgets that make room for people, on your team and in community.

  • Your real work moves.

    Before A good training that fades after the last session.

    After First steps for the plan or decision you brought in, named and owned together.

What it won’t fix

There’s a lot we can change from inside our organizations, from the tools we reach for and how we relate to communities, to how we care for one another and, harder but just as possible, who holds authority and how decisions get made. This series is about doing as much of that as we can.

We also want to be honest about where that ends. Like many Black radical, Black feminist and Indigenous thinkers and organizers, we don’t believe our institutions can transform themselves from within. Naming that limit is what lets us use our roles well. We take the most we can from inside, and use the room it buys to move money, resources and decisions toward the work communities are already leading, like mutual aid and community land trusts.

This isn’t for you if

  • You’re looking for a one-off training or a compliance module.
  • You want a checklist or a ready-made method to apply.
  • Your team can’t protect about an hour and a half a week each, for twelve weeks.
  • Your group doesn’t work closely together day to day, like a large network or a whole coalition.

How a series runs

Twelve weeks, built to fit around real workloads at about an hour and a half a week for each person, with up to 10 people who work closely together. Each team gets its own series, so we can go deep into how your team and your organization work. Here’s what you’ll have along the way, and keep afterward.

  • The video lessons, yours to keep. Come back to them whenever you need to, and use them to bring new colleagues into the work long after the series ends.

  • Four live sessions of 90 minutes, one every three weeks, led by Andréanne

  • Recordings with automatic transcripts, if your group is comfortable being recorded

  • A workbook with practices to return to whenever you’re shaping a plan, a process or a decision

  • A certificate of completion for each participant

Become a founding team

The first series will be small and shaped by the people in it, because so much of this learning comes from the learners themselves. We’ll welcome up to three founding teams to build it with us, each in its own series, starting between fall 2027 and early 2028. What you bring, what you push back on and what you try in your own work will shape every series that follows.

We’re looking for

A team of people who work closely together and engage communities in public health, planning, community development, climate, housing or nearby fields. That might be a program team in a nonprofit, an office in a public agency, or the steering group that shapes a coalition’s work. You’ll have a real plan, project or decision coming up between fall 2027 and early 2028, and be ready to sit with the uncomfortable parts of unlearning together.

What you’ll bring

Your questions, your real work and honest feedback as we go.

As thanks

A founding rate, lower than future series, and a follow-up session about three months after the series ends to look at what’s taken root and what you need next.

Questions people ask

Before you bring this to your team

Can I join on my own?

The series is for people who work closely together, because the habits we’re unlearning are shared, and so is the care it takes to change them. If you’re on your own, join the list and let us know. We’re listening for whether a series for individuals should come next.

Can our whole coalition or network join?

Usually not as a whole. This work goes deepest with people who plan and decide together week to week, and a coalition of many organizations rarely does. What tends to work is the core group that shapes the coalition’s work, like its steering or executive group. Together you can look at how the coalition itself makes decisions, and each of you will carry those questions back to your own organization. If your network is looking for something broader, join the list and tell us about it.

Is this a DEI training?

It shares some of what DEI work cares about, and you can file it that way if that’s how your budget works. The difference is that it goes into the work itself, how your team plans, decides, engages and governs, using a real project you bring in, with time between sessions to try things. The certificate of completion isn’t an accredited credential.

(Un)Learning, Sensemaking or Wayfinding?

The (Un)Learning Series is where a team questions the habits underneath the work, together. Sensemaking turns what you’ve already heard into a Compass or a Map your team can plan from. Wayfinding is ongoing advice for a team already practicing a new way of working. Many teams start with one and continue with another.

Let’s open what’s possible, together.

Questioning what we were taught is hard, and it’s harder alongside the people we work with every day. It’s also where the most lasting change begins. If your team is ready to be among the first to try it, and to help shape how it’s done, I’d love for you to join us.
