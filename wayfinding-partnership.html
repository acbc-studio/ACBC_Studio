For when you’ve started working differently, and it’s hard to hold.

Maybe this is where your team is right now.

What worked on the pilot hasn’t become how your team works yet.

You’ve run the co-design pilot, paid community members for their time, and brought partners in before the plan was written. It worked, and people noticed. Then the next project came with a tighter timeline and a smaller budget, and the old way crept back in. Not because anyone stopped caring, but because the new way still lives in a few people’s heads rather than in how your team works.

You’re the bridge, and the bridge carries the weight.

Your community partners trust you, so you’re the one who tells them what leadership won’t fund. Leadership trusts you, so you’re the one explaining why community members keep raising housing at a climate meeting. Every week is a quiet negotiation between what people asked for and what your institution can hold.

The hard calls come faster than you can think them through.

Do you push back on the timeline, or run the engagement knowing most of it is already decided? How do you word the board memo so it’s honest and still gets approved? These calls come every week, and there’s rarely anyone senior, who has made them before, to think them through with.

And often, it’s one person holding the line.

Usually it’s the one who cares most and has the least authority, asking “whose voice is missing?” until they’re tired of being the only one who asks. If they leave, much of what your team has learned leaves with them.

Why it keeps happening

None of this means you’re doing it wrong. You’re doing community-centered work inside structures that weren’t designed for it, often without the role, the authority or the budget it takes.

Most of this work is funded as a project, not as a practice. Grants pay for the workshop and the report in the middle. They rarely pay for the thinking before it, or for carrying what you heard into the next decision once the grant ends. So that work lands on you, on top of everything else.

What a Wayfinding Partnership is

Think of it as having a part-time senior engagement lead on your team: someone who has made these calls many times, without the cost or the long search of a full-time hire.

What I bring

We shape the approach together. When a piece needs senior hands, I make a starting point for you to finish, like an outline for next month’s session, the bones of the plan due to your funder, or a sketch of the figure for your board. I look at your drafts early, while changes are still easy, and I’m there to think it through with you before the moments that matter.

What stays with you

You’re the ones community members see, and the ones who’ll still hold the relationships next year. So you run the sessions, write the plans and make the final calls. That’s on purpose: when I leave, the know-how stays with the people who’ll keep using it.

How it works

There are four areas I advise on: how you listen, translate, show and decide. Each quarter, your team chooses one to focus on, so we can go deep enough to really change how the work gets done, rather than spreading thin across everything at once. In the second quarter, you can stay with the same area or move to a new one. The other areas aren’t off-limits in the meantime: if a board presentation comes up while you’re focused on Listen, we’ll still prepare for it together.

At the start of each quarter, we size the work to what’s on your desk: one big piece, like a strategic plan, or two or three smaller ones.

The four focus areas · One per quarter

Listen

Who shows up, and what happens when they do

You already know engagement should center the people most affected, start early and meet often. The hard part is doing it with a tight timeline, stretched partners and a decision that’s already half made. So we design each engagement around what’s honestly still open, and who most needs to be heard about it.

What changes Your next engagement is built for what it can really change, with the people most affected in the room, not just the ones who always come.

You’ll have An outreach plan, a partnership strategy and session designs.

Translate

Your communities’ words, carried into your plans

You know what people told you. The hard part is getting it through templates and reviews without it being trimmed, softened or filed as “out of scope.” I read your drafts early, while they’re still easy to change, and help you keep the honest point in words that can still get approved.

What changes Your communities would recognize themselves in the plan, and issues outside your lane get a clear answer instead of the “other” column.

You’ll have A plan or report, and a connections map.

Show

Making what you know easy to see

Your board, your funder and your communities hear the same findings very differently. We shape one core message with a version for each, and sketch the key figures together, so what you heard lands in one look instead of reading as anecdote next to the spreadsheet.

What changes The people who decide see the case clearly, and your communities get a report-back they’ll actually take in.

You’ll have Key figures, diagrams and tables for each audience.

Decide

Who gets a say in what happens next

You may already have an advisory group. The real question is what it gets to decide. We map who decides what right now, then pick one or two places where your communities could hold more of the decision, like setting priorities or where funds go. To choose this focus, you’ll need someone on your team who can change how decisions get made.

What changes At least one decision is shared with the people most affected, designed together and ready to try.

You’ll have A map of how decisions move, and a community-led structure ready to test.

Six months in practice

An example, not a client story

A housing coalition has a community engagement round in March and a plan due to their funder in June. We start in January with a starting map of what’s coming, and agree on two or three outcomes rooted in their mission and the shift they want in who holds knowledge and power. One of theirs: “the renters most affected by heat help decide where cooling resources go.”

Some moments from working together

Before the board

Being honest with the people above you builds trust, and it can cost you your standing. You need to make the case for paying community members for their time, so we work out how to say it so it lands, and what you’ll do if it doesn’t.

Before the funder report

Some of the program didn’t go as planned. We figure out how to show what changed, what you learned and what it means for what comes next, so progress is measured by what’s actually happening, not just by the targets set at the start.

Before the report-back

Some of what community members asked for didn’t make the plan. We plan how to tell them, what you’ll keep pushing for, and a standing way for them to reach you.

After it went sideways

Your anger knows exactly what’s wrong, and it’s often the first thing used to dismiss your ideas. When a meeting doesn’t go the way you hoped, we don’t brush it aside. We work out what it’s telling you and turn it into something clear you can bring back to the table.

What it won’t fix

A partnership won’t make your institution community-led in six months, and it can’t change the funding rules, timelines and structures around your team on its own. Reforming a system helps people now, and it can also keep a harmful one running, so we’ll stay honest about which one is happening.

What six months can give you is real first steps, a team that keeps practicing, and a clear record of where you ran into the edges of how your institution is set up. Knowing where those edges are tells you what to push on next, and gives you evidence for making the case.

This isn’t for you if

  • You’re just starting out with community engagement. Sensemaking or the Un-Learning Series might be a better first step.
  • You want someone to run your engagement or write your reports for you.
  • You have one event or one report coming up and nothing after it. Sensemaking might fit better.
  • Your team can’t protect about two hours a week.

Is this the right fit?

Wayfinding is for nonprofits, community-based organizations and close-knit coalitions working on health, climate, housing or community development. It works best for a team of two to five people who already do community-engaged work and want to go further than input, with one point person, something coming up in the next six months, and about two hours a week each to give it. It also asks for a willingness to share drafts before they’re polished, and to try things before you’re certain. I keep no more than four partnerships at a time.

A typical month

Two 90-minute working sessions with your team, and a shared space in between. Send a voice note, a draft or a quick video when something comes up, and I’ll reply within two working days, sooner around the moments we’ve planned for.

Questions people ask

Before you bring this to your team

Is this consulting?

A consultant usually takes the work away and hands back a result. Here, I advise on and help design your real work, but the work and the relationships stay with your team, and so does the know-how.

Can I work with you on my own?

Wayfinding is built for teams, because the habits we’re changing are shared. If you’re a director or lead who wants to start on your own first, let’s talk about it in a first conversation.

Sensemaking or Wayfinding?

Sensemaking is a project: it turns what you’ve already heard into a Compass or a Map your team can plan from. Wayfinding is ongoing advice for teams already doing this work, across how you listen, translate, show and decide. Many teams start with Sensemaking and continue with Wayfinding.

What happens after six months?

You keep your digital hub and everything in it. Some teams finish there, and others continue month to month with a new focus. We decide together in month six.

Let’s walk the next stretch together.

If a big plan, partnership or decision is coming up, the best time to start is now, before the timeline is set and while more is still open. And by the end, asking whose voice is missing won’t fall to one person. It’ll be how your team works.
