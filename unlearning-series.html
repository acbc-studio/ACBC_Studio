Un-Learning Series · Open what’s possible

The part of community engagement your degree didn’t teach.

For people in public health, planning, housing, climate and community development who came to this work to make change and keep bumping into the limits of how it gets done.

Over ten weeks, you and the people you work most closely with will examine the top-down conditioning so many of us carry, and how it shows up in the rooms you run and the decisions you make. Then you’ll practice a different relationship with your role, one where you walk alongside communities and answer to them, rather than deciding for them.

Maybe this is where you are right now.

A conversation that changed you became one line in a report.

You sat in a church basement while a grandmother explained why she stopped going to the clinic, and it took her an hour to tell it. By the third round of review, her hour had become “Participants cited access barriers.” You signed off on it and thought about her on the drive home.

Nothing was really up for discussion.

The flyer for the community meeting went out after the plan was drafted. In the second row, someone asked what was actually still open to change, and you realized you didn’t have a good answer. Back at the office, you wrote “robust community input” in the summary.

You were invited to the table to be the community.

You grew up a few blocks from the neighborhood the plan is about, so you’re the one asked to “bring the community voice.” Now people on both sides call you when something goes wrong, and some weeks you’re not sure which side you’re supposed to be on. You feel it in your shoulders by Friday.

You’ve read the books, and the work looks the same.

Your team read the book everyone was talking about, maybe because you suggested it. You agreed with every chapter. The next week, the planning meeting ran on the same template, with the same three options to vote on, and no one had the time, or a shared way, to bring what you’d read into the room.

You came into this work wanting things to be more just, and some days it feels like the job is asking you to speak for people instead of answering to them.

These ways of working were learned, which means they can be unlearned, together. That’s what the Un- is for.

Why it keeps happening

It starts long before any degree. As children, we learned that the person at the front of the classroom had the answers and our job was to get them right. We learned whose way of speaking sounded smart, which kinds of knowledge earned grades, and that a number usually wins over a story.

Then our training taught us to run an assessment, analyze the data and write the plan. Community engagement, if it came up at all, was a set of tools for gathering input, rarely relationships to tend or power to share. Many of the institutions that shaped us were designed to produce experts who decide for others.

Over the years, that settles into our minds and our bodies, until deferring to the expert view feels like common sense. None of us chose this, and most of us came to this work hoping to do the opposite. But the cost still falls hardest on the very communities our work is meant to serve, most often Black, Indigenous and other communities of color, whose knowledge is so often the first to be set aside.

That’s our conditioning

Experts decide.

Everyone else gets consulted, or worse, dismissed.

Black feminist, Black radical and Indigenous thinkers and movements named these patterns long ago. They’ve been practicing other ways of knowing and deciding for generations.

Our part, from inside our institutions, is to listen closely enough to act on what they teach.

A note from Andréanne

“I was taught that only what can be measured counts as knowledge, and that what I sensed didn’t.”

I grew up where science was the closest thing to faith, and I’ve spent my career in fields that trust what can be measured: a junior role at a city health department, a doctorate on this exact gap, and almost seven years leading projects with cities and nonprofits, in both seats, carrying what communities said and making the call. I was also adopted from China and raised by Québécois parents in the mostly white suburbs of Boston, so I’ve lived in the in-between my whole life.

That’s why I can walk this with your team. I know the expert role from the inside, so I can name the conditioning we carry without shaming anyone for it. And I know what it costs to be the one translating, so I can help your team hold rigor and other ways of knowing together, in language your institution will take seriously. I’m still un-learning, too, so I teach from inside that process, not above it.

What the series is

So what is our role, if not the expert?

On the surface, this series is about community engagement. Underneath, it’s about our relationship with our roles: what we’re actually responsible for, and who we’re accountable to, starting with the communities the work is for.

What many of us learned to doWhat answering to communities asks of us
Target, intervene and manage outcomesWalk alongside people, and let go of what isn’t ours to control
Gather input, then decideShare the decisions that matter most
Take stories and data for the reportMake sure knowledge flows back to the people it came from
Count only what fits the frame as evidenceCarry community knowledge whole, with its own rigor
Answer first to funders and the institutionAnswer to the people the work is for

Four conversations your team has never had the time to have

In each module, we’ll un-learn what we were taught and re-member what we learned to forget, as Black feminist scholar Cynthia B. Dillard puts it. You’ll learn from people who’ve spoken truth to power, from outside institutions and from within them, through work they’ve chosen to share. I’ll help your team carry it into your own work.

Weeks 2–3

Collective care

What would it mean to count care as real work?

You’ll leave with a new way of seeing care, for your team and your communities, and reflections on where rest, grief and fair pay could fit in your work.

We’ll un-learn the belief that output and deadlines come first, that the emotional labor of this work is a private cost, and that grief, exhaustion and what our bodies carry have no place at work.

We’ll re-member care as part of the work, as communities have long held it, from the Black Panthers’ survival programs and mutual aid to disability justice’s collective access.

Learning from the Combahee River Collective, the Black Panther Party’s survival programs, Sins Invalid, Leah Lakshmi Piepzna-Samarasinha, adrienne maree brown and Resmaa Menakem.

Weeks 4–5

Living memory

Whose knowledge guides the work, and whose is missing?

You’ll leave with a wider sense of what counts as knowledge, including ways of knowing you were never taught, and language to make room for them in your work.

We’ll un-learn taking people’s stories and disappearing until the next assessment, holding all knowledge to one standard of evidence, and guarding relationships with communities as ours to hold, the way scarcity teaches us to.

We’ll re-member knowledge as something held in relationship, by staying connected between cycles, bringing back what people shared, and honoring lived and embodied experience as evidence.

Learning from Linda Tuhiwai Smith, Shawn Wilson, Patricia Hill Collins, Densho’s community archive and Indigenous data sovereignty movements.

Weeks 6–7

Shared power

Who decides now, and who could?

You’ll leave with a clearer view of who holds power in your work, and of how people have long shared it, with questions to bring to your next decisions.

We’ll un-learn our conditioning toward control, from a few people holding the final say to bringing community in once the plan is set.

We’ll re-member power as relationship and reciprocity, from Ella Baker’s group-centered leadership and Black cooperatives to the Zapatistas’ leading by obeying and Indigenous resurgence.

Learning from Ella Baker, Larry Itliong and the Filipino farmworkers of Delano, Jessica Gordon Nembhard, the Zapatistas, Leanne Betasamosake Simpson and INCITE!

Weeks 8–9

Radical imagination

What becomes possible when “realistic” isn’t the limit?

You’ll leave with room to imagine beyond what’s called realistic, and ideas for what could shift inside your walls and alongside community-led work.

We’ll un-learn the single story of progress, and the sense that funders and markets set the limits of what’s possible.

We’ll re-member that these limits weren’t always here, drawing on ways of living beyond them, from ancestral practice to the community-led and abolitionist futures people are building now.

Learning from Robin D.G. Kelley, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Mariame Kaba, Grace Lee Boggs, Yuri Kochiyama and Faranak Miraftab.

Ten weeks, with a live session every other week

Ten weeks, for up to 10 people who work closely together. We open with a short form and a live session, so I can understand what your team is working through and shape the series around it. Each module then pairs video lessons you watch at your own pace with a live conversation about what they mean for your work. In the closing session, we look back at what’s shifted and put together a light action plan for what comes next.

$9,500 per team of up to 10 people.

Your team is its own cohort: every live session is just you, never mixed with other organizations.

What you keep

  • The video lessons. Come back to them whenever you need to, and use them to bring new colleagues into the work long after the series ends.

  • A workbook to reflect in as you watch, with prompts to return to whenever you’re shaping a plan, a process or a decision.

  • A light action plan your team puts together in the closing session.

  • A certificate of completion for each participant. It isn’t an accredited credential.

What changes

You have words for what you’ve always known.

When a board or funder asks for rigor, your team has the language and sources to make room for stories, lived experience and other ways of knowing, so what communities tell you holds its ground, and your team is seen as knowing how to do this work.

You’re finally on the same side of it.

The person doing the work and the person leading it stop seeing it from two sides. The team sees what its leader is working against, its leader sees what the team carries, and who’s missing and who decides become questions you ask together.

Care stops being an afterthought.

Your team can talk openly about rest, grief, fair pay and the emotional labor this work asks, for yourselves and for the community members you ask to show up, and knows where to start making room for them.

Your commitments finally show up in the everyday work.

The equity commitments your organization made start to show up in how your team plans, decides and engages day to day, not only on paper. Your team can feel it, and so can the communities you serve.

What it won’t fix

There’s a lot we can change from inside our organizations: the tools we reach for, how we relate to communities, how we care for one another, and, harder but just as possible, who holds authority.

But like many Black radical, Black feminist and Indigenous thinkers and organizers, I don’t believe our institutions can transform themselves from within. Dismantling these systems is work shared by many, and bigger than any one series.

So this series focuses on what you can disrupt from the inside, and on where money, resources and decisions could move into the hands of communities already building their own futures.

This isn’t for you if

  • You’re looking for a one-off training or a compliance module.
  • You want answers handed to you, rather than questions to work through together.
  • Your team can’t commit to a live session every other week, plus videos in between.
  • You’re a large network or coalition, rather than one team that works together day to day.
  • You need this work to avoid the word equity.

Questions people ask

Before you bring this to your team

Can I join on my own?

The series is for people who work closely together, because what we’re unlearning is shared, and so is the care it takes to change it. If you’re on your own, join the list and let me know. I’m listening for whether a series for individuals should come next, one held outside our institutions, where people can un-learn on their own terms.

Should the person who leads our team take part?

Yes, if they can. This goes deepest when the people who lead a team un-learn alongside it, rather than sending the team on its own. It also shows the team that questioning how things are done is welcome, starting with the people who lead it.

Is this a DEI training?

It shares some of what DEI work cares about, and you can file it that way if that’s how your budget works. The difference is that it goes into the work itself, how your team plans, decides, engages and governs, starting from what your team is working through, with time between sessions to reflect.

What can our live sessions focus on?

Each live session starts from what that module raises for your work, and we shape it around what your team needs most. That might be a plan, an engagement round or a decision you have coming up, practicing how to design and hold engagement, or making room for care, rest and fair pay in how your team works.

Un-Learning, Sensemaking or Wayfinding?

The Un-Learning Series is where a team steps back and questions what we were taught, underneath the work, together. Sensemaking puts it into practice on a plan you’re working on now, turning what you’ve heard into a Compass or a Map. Wayfinding is ongoing advice, week by week, for a team already practicing a new way of working. Many teams start with one and continue with another.

Do you use AI?

A bit, yes, as a tool. It helps me edit the video lessons and slides, and design the workbook. However, nothing is ever fully generated by AI. Anything I make just for your team during the series can be made without it, if you’d prefer. I’m happy to share a modified quote for that.

Let’s open what’s possible, together.

Questioning what we were taught is hard, especially alongside the people we work with every day, and it takes grace for the mistakes we’ll make along the way.

But every round done the old way costs something. Partners who’ve trusted you for years come a little less often. The people on your team who sit across from communities feel it first, and some of them won’t stay.

Right now there’s a clear public call for institutions to answer to the communities they serve. The next best time to start is the plan already on your desk.
