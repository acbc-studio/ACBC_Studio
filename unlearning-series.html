(Un)Learning Series · Open what’s possible

The part of community engagement your degree didn’t teach.

For people in public health, planning and community development who came to this work to make change, and keep bumping into the limits of how it gets done.

Over twelve weeks, your team or coalition will look closely at the top-down habits so many of us picked up along the way, and practice a different relationship with your role, one where you walk alongside communities and answer to them, rather than deciding for them.

Maybe this is where you are right now.

  • Turning what community members told you into a logic model, and feeling what got sanded off along the way.
  • Bringing a community member’s story to the table and being asked, “But what does the data say?”
  • Going back to the same neighborhood for another survey, and hearing, “We already told you.”
  • Inviting people in after the plan, the program or the service was already decided.
  • Watching a grant or a project end just as people were starting to trust you.
  • Running the same workshop again, whatever the question or the community.
  • Being the one expected to bring “the community voice” because you’re from there, or look like you might be, and carrying the weight of both worlds.
  • Leading a team that wants to work differently, while answering to a council, a board or a funder that doesn’t.

You’ve learned to speak the institution’s language beautifully, and you can feel what gets lost every time you do. You came into this work wanting things to be more just, and some days it feels like the job is asking you to manage people instead. Maybe you’re the one on your team who keeps asking whose voice is missing, and you’re a little tired of being that person. Or maybe you’ve read the books and sat through the trainings, and you still walk into tomorrow’s meeting not quite sure what to do differently.

These ways of working were learned, which means they can be unlearned, together. That’s what the (Un) is for.

Where these habits come from

Most of our training taught us to run an assessment, analyze the data and write the plan, then come back and do it all again next cycle. When community engagement came up at all, it was usually as a set of tools for gathering input, like a survey, a focus group or a public meeting, and rarely as relationships to tend or power to share.

That’s no accident. Many of the schools and institutions that shaped us were built to produce experts who decide on behalf of others. And some of it we absorbed long before any degree, growing up in Western society and learning whose word counts, who gets treated as an authority and who gets trusted to decide for themselves. Those lessons land hardest on Black, Indigenous and other communities of color, immigrants, and low-income and queer communities, whose knowledge is so often the first to be dismissed.

Underneath it all are colonial, capitalist and neoliberal ways of seeing the world. They’re so familiar they’re like the water we swim in, so it helps to see what they look like day to day.

Colonial

Deciding about people, without them

  • Surveying and studying the same neighborhoods again and again, while the people who live there rarely decide how their data is used, or see what came of it.
  • Calling a neighborhood “blighted,” and letting a consultant’s assessment outweigh what the people who live there know about it.

Capitalist

Valuing what can be counted, sold or reported

  • Counting success in units built and people served, while the relationships that made any of it possible never show up in the budget or the report.
  • Thanking community members with a gift card, if they’re paid at all, for knowledge that took a lifetime to build.
  • Watching redlined neighborhoods get “revitalized” once land values rise, while people who have lived there for generations are priced out. Who loses and who profits has always followed lines of race, which is what Cedric Robinson called racial capitalism.

Neoliberal

Turning shared conditions into personal problems

  • Asking people to eat better or build an emergency kit when the problem is food access or flood risk, which hands shared conditions to the people living with them.
  • Designing a program around what you can count within a one-year grant, then starting over just as trust begins to form.
  • Competing for contracts with the organizations you should be building with, and hearing “that’s just how funding works.”

These patterns also shape what we count as evidence. It makes sense that our fields lean on being evidence-based, since nobody wants decisions that are uninformed or corrupt. But somewhere along the way, evidence came to mean numbers, or stories reshaped to fit the same frames, and that quietly decides whose knowledge gets to guide the work.

None of this critique is new. Black feminist, Black radical and Indigenous thinkers, scholars and organizers, alongside liberation, anticolonial and decolonial movements, named these patterns long ago. Their ways of knowing, deciding and caring for one another, from ancestral practices to organizing happening right now, are very much alive, even when the institutions we work in don’t see them or take them seriously. Our part, from inside those institutions, is to listen closely enough to act on what they teach.

From expert to steward

At its heart, this series is about building a new relationship with our roles. What are we actually responsible for? Who are we accountable to? And how do the tools, plans and activities we use every day, and the way decisions get made around us, need to change to match?

What many of us learned to doWhat stewardship asks of us
Target, intervene and manage outcomes→Walk alongside people, and let go of what isn’t ours to control
Gather input, then decide→Share the decisions that matter most
Take stories and data for the report→Make sure knowledge flows back to the people it came from
Count only what fits the frame as evidence→Carry community knowledge whole, with its own rigor
Come back each assessment cycle→Hold relationships with communities and partners between the cycles
Answer first to funders and leadership→Answer to the people the work is for

What we’ll explore

Here’s our early outline. The series will take shape from January to May 2027 with input from people on the list and our founding teams, so the themes and their order may still change. Each theme runs for three weeks, moving from short lessons you take at your own pace, to a practice you try in your own work, to a live session together. Every idea comes with its source named, so you can follow the thread home.

Before we begin. A short questionnaire helps your team name the plan, project or decision you’ll carry through the series, so every session is about your real work.

Weeks 1–3

Collective care

What would it mean to count care as real work?

We’ll unlearn the belief that only output counts and the deadline comes first, which is why care ends up after hours, on the same few people, and why timelines follow grant cycles instead of people.

We’ll practice building care into the work itself, for communities and for your team, with timelines that follow community rhythms, budgets that make room for fair pay and childcare, and meetings where people can say they’re stretched.

You’ll leave with a clear picture of who’s carrying what in your own project, and one change to share that load.

Learning from the Combahee River Collective, the Black Panther Party’s survival programs, disability justice organizers like Sins Invalid, and adrienne maree brown.

Weeks 4–6

Living memory

Whose knowledge guides the work, and whose is missing?

We’ll unlearn taking people’s stories and data and disappearing until the next assessment, holding all knowledge to one standard of evidence, and treating community rhythms like seasons, holidays and grief as delays to manage.

We’ll practice staying in relationship with communities and community-based organizations between cycles, making sure what people share comes back to them, and honoring stories, elders’ knowledge and lived experience as evidence in their own right.

You’ll leave with the language and sources to stand up for community knowledge in rooms that ask for rigor.

Learning from Linda Tuhiwai Smith, Shawn Wilson on relational accountability, Patricia Hill Collins on lived experience as knowledge, and Indigenous data sovereignty movements.

Weeks 7–9

Shared power

Who decides now, and who could?

We’ll unlearn the habit of control, and the governance that keeps it in place, from a few people holding the final say to bringing community in only once the plan is already set.

We’ll practice mapping who decides at each step of your project and inside your organization, learning from other ways of governing like community boards with real authority, participatory budgeting and consensus, and building community in from the start of the next cycle. The first time will almost always feel too late. Once it’s built in, the next time won’t be.

You’ll leave with one decision, in your project or your organization, that your team is ready to share, and a plan for sharing it.

Learning from Ella Baker, the Zapatistas’ principle of leading by obeying, Paulo Freire, and INCITE!’s critique of the nonprofit industrial complex.

Weeks 10–12

Radical imagination

What becomes possible when “realistic” isn’t the limit?

We’ll unlearn the single story of progress that decides what a good future looks like for everyone, the sense that funders and markets set the limits of what’s possible, and the hope that our institutions can carry transformation on their own.

We’ll practice imagining your work differently together, then naming what you can change from inside and how you can support the community-led work happening outside your walls.

You’ll leave with your team’s first steps, inside and outside your organization, named and owned together.

Learning from Robin D.G. Kelley, Ruth Wilson Gilmore, Mariame Kaba and Faranak Miraftab.

Twelve weeks in practice

An example, not a client story

A county health department team is starting its next community health improvement plan in the fall, and brings it into the series as their project.

Collective care

They notice that two people carry every community relationship, and that the timeline runs straight through harvest season for the farmworker families they most need to hear from. They move the listening sessions and budget for childcare and fair pay.

Living memory

They find three years of input from past assessments that never went back to the people who gave it, so they plan a report-back before asking anyone anything new.

Shared power

They map who decides at each step and choose one decision to share. Community members on the steering group will set the plan’s priorities, not just review them.

Radical imagination

They name what they’ll change inside the department, and how they’ll support the tenant union already organizing on housing conditions outside it.

What it won’t fix

There’s a lot we can change from inside our organizations, and this series is about doing as much of it as we can. We can reshape the tools, plans and activities we reach for. We can relate to communities differently and care for one another better. And, harder but just as possible, we can start shifting who holds authority and how decisions get made, even in places that weren’t built to share power.

We also want to be honest about where that ends. Audre Lorde said the master’s tools might let us beat him at his own game for a time, but they won’t bring about genuine change. Like many Black radical, Black feminist and Indigenous thinkers and organizers, we don’t believe our institutions can transform themselves from within.

Naming that limit is what lets us use our roles well. We take the most we can from inside, and we use the room it buys to hold the door open for communities and the work they’re already leading, like insurgent planning, mutual aid and community land trusts, moving money, resources and decisions their way so they stay in community hands.

This isn’t for you if

  • You’re looking for a one-off training or a compliance module.
  • You want a checklist or a ready-made method to apply.
  • Your team can’t protect about an hour and a half a week each, for twelve weeks.
  • You’re on your own. The first series is for teams and coalitions, but join the list and tell us.

How a series runs

The series runs for twelve weeks and is built to fit around real workloads, at about an hour and a half a week for each person. Up to 20 people from your team or coalition can take part. Here’s what you’ll have along the way, and keep afterward.

  • The video lessons, yours to keep. Watch them at your own pace, come back to them whenever you need to, and use them to bring new colleagues into the work long after the series ends. A lot of trainings keep their material locked away. This one is meant to stay with you.

  • Four live sessions of 90 minutes, one every three weeks, led by Andréanne with a studio co-facilitator

  • Recordings of the live sessions with automatic transcripts, if your group is comfortable being recorded

  • A workbook you fill in as you go, with practices to return to whenever you’re shaping a plan, a process or a decision

  • A certificate of completion for each participant

Founding teams for fall 2027

The first series will be small and shaped with the people in it, because so much of this learning comes from the learners themselves. We’ll welcome up to three founding teams who want to build it with us, bringing their questions, their work and honest feedback as we go.

We’re looking for

Teams or coalitions who engage communities in public health, planning, community development, climate, housing or nearby fields, who have a real plan, project or decision coming up in fall 2027, and who are ready to sit with the uncomfortable parts of unlearning together.

As thanks

A founding rate, lower than future series, and a follow-up session about three months after the series ends to look at what’s taken root and what you need next.

Questions people ask

Before you bring this to your team

Can I join on my own?

The first series is for teams and coalitions, because the habits we’re unlearning are shared, and so is the care it takes to change them. If you’re on your own, join the list and let us know. We’re listening for whether a series for individuals should come next.

Is this a DEI training?

It shares some of what DEI work cares about, and you can file it that way if that’s how your budget works. The difference is that it goes into the work itself, how your team plans, decides, engages and governs, using a real project you bring in, with time between sessions to try things.

Is there a certificate?

Yes, each participant receives a certificate of completion. It isn’t an accredited credential or a formal certificate program. If you hold a credential that needs continuing-education credits, like CPH, AICP or CHES, let us know when you join the list, since it helps us decide what to offer in future series.

(Un)Learning, Sensemaking or Wayfinding?

The (Un)Learning Series is where a team questions the habits underneath the work, together. Sensemaking turns what you’ve already heard into a Compass or a Map your team can plan from. Wayfinding is ongoing advice for a team already practicing a new way of working. Many teams start with one and continue with another.
