You already know what communities tell you isn’t making it into the plan.

Maybe it’s the listening session you helped design, knowing the decisions had already been made.

You smiled through it, thanked everyone for coming, and sat in your car for a while before you could drive home.

The report you sent back to community that didn’t quite sound like what they said. You know who will read it.

Some of them you’ve known for years, and you know they’ll notice.

The meeting where you saw what was about to go wrong, and chose your words so carefully that no one heard them.

You spent the evening rewriting what you should have said.

The community meeting where someone stood up and asked why nothing ever changes. You knew every reason: the budget, the timeline, the vote that went the other way.

None of them felt like an answer, and none of them showed how hard you’d fought.

Wherever you sit, you’re answering to more people than anyone sees.

The communities you serve, your partners, the people who fund and approve the work. Most days, the tension between them sits with you. You’re tired in a way a weekend doesn’t fix. And some nights, thinking about the people who still trust you to carry what they said, you wonder whether you did enough, whether you pushed hard enough.

The walls you keep running into were built long before you got here.

Most of us were trained, in school, at work and in the culture around us, to believe rigor means distance, and that care, relationship and other ways of knowing are lovely extras. Then we’re asked to do justice work inside institutions built for control and extraction, with equity bolted on afterward. The communities you serve have been living with those walls for generations.

Some part of you already knows another way is possible. I suspect that’s why you’re here.

Why I work with you, not for you

Another way begins in the work already on your desk.

When you’re this stretched, the usual answers are another training or book club, or a consultant who’ll take it off your plate. They all make sense. But a training doesn’t follow you into next month’s listening session, and a consultant leaves with the relationships and the know-how. What changes this work is learned inside it: in the room when a tenant tells you what the heat in her apartment does to her kids’ sleep, and in the meeting where the heat plan gets decided, with her words still whole on the table.

Our practice

Everyday Justice

Justice is something we learn by practicing it, alongside the communities our work serves. It takes shape in who gets heard, whose knowledge guides a plan, whether people hear back about what came of it, and who has a say over resources and decisions. It doesn’t need to wait for the next strategic plan. It goes into the agenda, the template and the decision on your desk this month.

Everyday Justice guides how we work. We return to four connected dimensions to question familiar ways of working and put our commitments into practice.

  • Ground

    We begin with the place, its history, community knowledge and the assumptions each of us brings to the work.

  • Weave

    We tend relationships with care, so they can outlast any one project, and learn to share responsibility and navigate differences.

  • Spark

    We question familiar approaches and try new ways of working, guided by what communities know, need and imagine.

  • Stitch

    We explore how budgets, policies and decision-making structures need to change for community priorities to carry real influence.

Why me

I’ve also learned it from inside institutions. I started in a junior role at a city health department, studied this gap for my doctorate, and have spent almost seven years since leading projects with cities, nonprofits and the partnerships between them. I’ve sat in both seats: the one carrying what communities said, and the one making the call.

So I can see the unwritten rules that pull a team back to the old way, translate between what communities know and what your institution will act on, and hold rigor and care together. I’m beside you at every step. The relationships stay yours, long after I’ve gone.

Our part in something much bigger

I won’t promise that any of this transforms a system on its own. Audre Lorde reminded us that the master’s tools will never dismantle the master’s house, and Robin D.G. Kelley shows how the boldest visions of freedom come from the movements of the people most harmed.

That doesn’t make our part small. From inside, we hold the budgets, the data, the invitations and the pen on the plan, and each of them can open a door or close one. Keeping a door open in a structure built to close it is hard, unglamorous work, and it takes courage.

My teacher, Dr. Joey Liu, describes three ways to act on systems: we disrupt within institutions, divest from them where we can, and join others in dismantling them over time. These offers live mostly in the first: changing how decisions get made from the inside, one decision at a time.

You don’t have to change everything at once. Start by disrupting how the next decision gets made.

Disrupting can be small: asking who isn’t in the room yet, leaving one question truly open, going back to people before asking again. Hope can be as steady and ordinary as the next decision we make with them, not for them.

Many of us committed to equity a long time ago, some of us in school, many in the summer of 2020. Since then, decisions have kept getting made the old way. Each one costs something: people give their time and see little come back, plans have to be redone, and there’s a little less trust to build on next time.

It works the other way too. Each decision made with communities makes the next one easier: partners who come back, a team that knows how, trust that builds on itself. But it only builds from the day you start. Until then, the next session, the next budget and the next draft get decided without them.

Your next decision is already on the calendar. Let’s make it with your communities, not for them.
