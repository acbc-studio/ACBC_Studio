Hi, I’m Andréanne Chu.

If you’ve found your way here, I suspect you came to this work for your communities, and somewhere along the way began to feel the distance between what your institution means to do and what it’s able to do. I feel it too. I’ve come to see that distance as a question about knowledge, about whose gets believed and who gets to take part. It has run through my whole life, long before I had words for it. Here’s how it found me, and how it shaped the way I work with people like you.

Where it began

I’m a Chinese-Québécois-American adoptee who grew up in a very white, wealthy suburb of Boston, with real privilege and a family safety net. In our home, science was the closest thing to faith, the kind that measures and proves. Being smart wasn’t just something I was. It was how I understood my worth.

I started school as a free-spirited girl, not yet shaped by what girls were supposed to be. Then the bullying began, and I learned to make myself smaller. I noticed early how power moved and who got to belong, and I could feel when I was treated unfairly, and differently, long before I understood why. The anger had to go somewhere, and sometimes it spilled over. When I spoke up about what felt unjust, my anger became the problem. I was too sensitive, too reactive, too much.

So I did what so many of us learn to do, and contorted myself to be let in. If what I felt didn’t count as knowledge, my mind would have to. I learned to lead with what I could explain and prove, and to quiet what I sensed but couldn’t yet put into words. I hoped that being recognized for my intelligence would mean being accepted. But being valued for my mind wasn’t the same as being welcomed as my whole self.

Between countries, languages and bodies

As an adult, I’ve made a home in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom and now Spain, living between English, French, Spanish and Catalan. Each move meant learning more than a language or a set of rules. It meant figuring out expectations that often went unspoken, because they were familiar to the people already there. Moving between places helped me see how much navigating a system depends on knowledge it assumes you already have, and how hard that knowledge is to notice from inside.

Living with a dynamic disability made those assumptions visible in another way. It wasn’t only about knowing how things worked, but whether I could move through them as expected. My capacity changes, while so much around me is organized around a steady level of energy, output and speed. Understanding what was expected didn’t necessarily make it possible.

Across countries, languages and bodies, I came to recognize how easily one way of moving through the world becomes the default. I once fell with my rollator at a curb cut that was almost low enough, built with good intentions, just not for a body like mine. What seems straightforward from one position can take enormous effort from another, effort that often goes unseen.

Never fully inside, never fully outside.

Each of these experiences put me at an edge, where I learned to see what others miss. So much of my work is making that visible, so we can change it together.

Learning the language of the work

In 2017, I brought those ways of seeing into public health, starting at a city health department outside Boston, working on food justice. I came because I could sense how food, housing, income and power were tangled together, long before I could draw it in a logic model. Inside local government, I also learned to speak the institution’s language, from grant cycles and indicators to the careful wording that gets a plan approved.

In 2020, I started my own practice, ACBC Consulting, supporting cities, coalitions and nonprofits in public health and climate resilience. I built logic models and theories of change, designed engagement and translated what communities told us into plans and funding. The fluency I’d built as a girl became my job, and I was good at it. At its best, that work helped community priorities reshape the approach. Medford’s Climate Resilience Community Liaison Program, which put belonging at the heart of climate resilience, is work I’m still proud of.

And yet, the more fluent I became, the more I noticed what got lost in translation. When community priorities got smaller on their way into a plan, I pushed for what I could, called what didn’t land a lesson learned and carried it into the next project. Being in the room mattered, I told myself. But the next project came, and I kept meeting the same limits.

The crack

I began to wonder whether the ways I’d learned to plan, gather evidence and measure progress were also limiting what could change, and whether the learning that mattered most could ever fit in the documents we produced.

That frightened me. These were the tools I knew, and part of what had earned me credibility. Questioning them meant questioning my own practice. So I began listening again to what I sensed, even when I couldn’t yet put it into words.

What changed me

Then, during my PhD in Barcelona, came a project I co-led with community members in Everett, Massachusetts, where conversations about extreme heat and climate resilience kept turning to housing, rent and displacement. Working multiple jobs and stretched financially, people had little time or energy to care for their families, connect with neighbors or organize together.

At times, there was concern that we were drifting off topic. I’d learned to trust connections before I could explain them, so I encouraged us to follow where community members led. Housing, care and the conditions for building collective power weren’t distractions from resilience. They were central to it.

Following where community members led changed me, in a way I couldn’t have learned from reading about it. We had come asking how people could cope with the heat. They showed us the question itself was too small. I’d spent years asking who benefits from a plan. Everett taught me to ask an earlier question: who got to define the problem in the first place?

What I’d want you to know

Some of what I once thought I had to overcome has become part of what I bring to the work.

From being called too much

The sensitivity and anger at injustice I was told to quiet help me notice what others overlook. I’m learning to trust them again. What you sense in your body, before you have words for it, can help you name what needs to change.

From contorting to belong

I set aside parts of myself to be taken seriously. Reclaiming them means bringing more than institutional fluency to the work, like intuition, lived experience and care. You don’t have to choose between being rigorous and bringing your whole self.

From living outside the design

When a system leaves people out, it’s the design we need to question, not their ability to fit. A curb cut or a meeting format can decide who takes part. What you’ve had to navigate can help you see what needs redesigning, so more people have a way in.

From years of translating

Moving between communities and institutions taught me to hear where meanings diverge. Conflict, held with care, is how we learn to live together. Your fluency across worlds can help people find common ground without erasing differences or tensions.

What I’m working toward

So much of my story has been about finding a way to belong. I want a future where belonging doesn’t require us to contort ourselves, and where caring for one another isn’t squeezed into whatever time is left.

I carry a vision of phoenix communities, bright lights rising and connected like constellations. Places where care is sustained, communities hold power, and the knowledge passed between generations helps guide what comes next. When I picture them, this is what I see.

  • Collective care

    Care belongs to the whole community. Neighbors mother each other’s children and elders, and that tending is honored as real work.

  • Shared power

    The people closest to a place set the questions, hold their own knowledge and decide what its streets, homes and services become.

  • Living memory

    The past is a living teacher. Stories are retold, survival is celebrated, and what a place has lived through guides each new choice.

  • Radical imagination

    Everyone gets to dream out loud. Neighbors imagine together what their block could become, and start building it now.

Liberation, for me, looks this ordinary.

The work we do together is one part of moving toward those futures. We practice what we can now, while working to change the conditions that stand in the way.

The studio

ACBC began as my consulting practice and has grown into a studio, a place of practice where we make things together, learn by doing and try again. Each part of that vision asks for creative work, from designing collective care to organizing community power.

I lead the work, with a circle of collaborators who join as each project needs, like a co-facilitator who helps hold the room or an experience designer who shapes how a session feels. You’ll meet them in Un-Learning Series live sessions. A Wayfinding Partnership is just the two of us.

With gratitude

I’m grateful to Joey Liu, who helped me find the story beneath the résumé, and to see my work as teaching and as gathering my people. This website exists because of her. I’m grateful, too, to those who made me feel I belonged in this work. Patrice C. Williams walked beside me in Everett, where so much changed for me. And at the Barcelona Lab for Urban Environmental Justice, my intellectual home, Isabelle Anguelovski, Helen V. S. Cole, Margarita Triguero-Mas and Panagiota Kotsila have shown me that rigor and care belong together.

Others have reminded me how I belong on this earth, through teachings they chose to share, though I’ve met them only on the page. bell hooks showed me that learning can be a practice of freedom. Linda Tuhiwai Smith keeps me asking whose questions our tools serve. adrienne maree brown reminds me that change moves at the speed of trust. Their teachings grow from generations of Black feminist, Black radical and Indigenous thinkers, organizers and communities. I try to receive these learnings with care, and to point back to where they come from.

And I’m grateful to the land, though my relationship with it is still taking shape. Jiujiang (九江), where my life began, a place I’m still coming to know. Greater Boston, Massachusett, Pawtucket and Naumkeag homelands, where I grew up. Québec, from Tiohtià:ke to Nionwentsïo, the lands of the Kanien’kehá:ka, Wendat and other Nations, where my family is rooted. Olivella, in Catalonia, where I live now, farther than ever from the places that made me. In North America, I reckon with being a settler. In Europe, I try not to look away from unspoken colonial histories.

Let’s get to know each other

If parts of my story sound like yours, we’re probably meant to find each other.

Maybe you’re finding ways to bring your commitments to care, belonging and community power into your everyday work. I’m right there with you. With your rigor and your care, what you can prove and what you’re still finding words for, I’d love to connect.

With love + solidarity,Andréanne Chu
